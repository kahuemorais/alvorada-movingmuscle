## Does a roof have to face south?

No. South is the best direction in the northern hemisphere, but a roof facing east or west still produces most of what a south roof does, and a north roof produces more than most people expect. Measured with the European Commission's PVGIS for Phoenix, one kilowatt of panels at a 20 degree tilt makes 1,771 kilowatt hours a year facing south, 1,527 facing east, 1,508 facing west, and 1,197 facing north.

## Which direction is best, and by how much?

South, and the gap is smaller than the usual advice suggests. A west-facing roof gives up about 15% of the south roof's annual production, an east-facing roof about 14%, and a north-facing roof about 32%. Those percentages are the honest way to read it: direction moves the count of panels a roof needs, it rarely decides whether solar works at all.

## Does the tilt matter as much as the direction?

Less, in Phoenix. The same kilowatt laid flat instead of tilted 20 degrees south makes about 1,568 kilowatt hours a year, which is about 12% below the tilted array. Flat roof installations in Arizona give up less than homeowners expect, and a tilted row on a flat roof recovers most of the difference.

## What about shade?

Shade costs more than direction does. A panel in shadow produces less, and in a system wired in series it can pull down the panels next to it, which is why a single shading chimney can matter more than a roof that faces west. Systems that use module-level electronics limit the damage to the shaded panel. Nothing on the roof can be judged from a map: if a tree, a parapet or a water heater casts a shadow on the array in the afternoon, that has to be seen on site.

## Does the Phoenix heat cost production?

Yes, and the estimate already accounts for it. Panels lose efficiency as their cells get hot, and Phoenix roofs get hot. Instead of modelling temperature, dust and inverter losses one at a time, the city page folds all three into a single performance factor of 80%, which is the number that keeps the production estimate from being optimistic.

## What if the roof faces the wrong way?

The system gets bigger for the same coverage, or covers less of the bill for the same count. Both are visible on the city page, because the count is what changes. The one case worth a different conversation is a north roof shaded in the afternoon, where the honest answer may be fewer panels on the good plane rather than a system sized around a roof that will never produce its share.

## Where do these numbers come from?

The yield figures come from PVGIS, queried for Phoenix at one kilowatt, a 14% loss and the tilt named above, and the Department of Energy's homeowner guide covers how orientation and shade enter a system design. The performance factor and the panel count come from the city page, computed from the data of that city.
