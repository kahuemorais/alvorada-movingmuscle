## Where does the extra power go?

To the grid, and the utility records it on your account as a credit. Power your house is not using at that moment leaves through the meter, and the utility applies it against what you draw later, usually at night and in the winter.

## Does the utility pay you for it?

No. Credits roll forward against future bills and do not become a payment, so a system that produces more than the house uses does not send a check. That single rule is why savings cap at the size of the bill, and why the calculator shows the cap instead of the production.

## How does the cap show up in the numbers?

On the Phoenix page, a three-bedroom house with a $220 bill covered at 100% takes 21 panels, which produce $221.13 worth of electricity a month at $0.15 per kilowatt hour. The savings shown are $220, and the remaining $1.13 is credited forward rather than paid. Cover 80% of the same bill with 17 panels and there is no surplus at all: production is worth $179.01, below the bill, and every kilowatt hour is consumed on site.

## Why size a system below the bill, then?

Because production above the bill is worth less than production below it. A kilowatt hour that offsets a kilowatt hour you would have bought is worth the full retail rate. A kilowatt hour exported is worth whatever the utility's rules say it is worth, which is generally a lower credit, and it never becomes cash. Sizing to the bill keeps as much production as possible on the retail side of that line.

## Does a surplus mean the money was wasted?

Not entirely. Exported power credits forward, so it offsets the night and winter usage of the same house, which a system sized exactly to the summer bill would otherwise buy from the utility. The surplus only becomes waste when it is large enough that the credits never get used, which is a real risk for a house with a small bill and a roof that holds a lot of panels.

## Who decides what an export is worth?

The state and the utility, not the installer and not the panel. The rate for exported power, the period credits carry over, and whether credits survive the end of a billing year all come from the utility's rate schedule and the state's rules, and they differ from one utility to the next. The Energy Information Administration keeps the state-level electricity data, and the document that governs a specific house is the tariff sheet of its utility.

## What should the estimate tell you about this?

Which side of the line each kilowatt hour lands on. That means four numbers: the bill, the coverage chosen, the production the system makes, and the savings after the cap is applied. When a quote shows production and monthly savings that are equal, ask what rate it credited the surplus at, because at retail rate that is only true if nothing is exported.

## Where do these figures come from?

The production, the savings and the cap come from the Phoenix page, computed from that city's data file. The Department of Energy's homeowner guide covers the grid connection and the metering side, and the Energy Information Administration's data browser is the public source for state-level electricity data.
