## What does the utility rate change?

Everything on the savings side of an estimate. Monthly savings are the kilowatt hours the system produces multiplied by the rate you pay for a kilowatt hour, capped at the bill the system is replacing. The hardware sets the production; the tariff sets what that production is worth.

## How does the arithmetic work on the Phoenix page?

The Phoenix file uses the Arizona Public Service residential tariff of $0.15 per kilowatt hour. A 17-panel system there produces 1,193 kilowatt hours a month, which is $179.01 of electricity at that rate, against a bill of $220. Cover the whole bill with 21 panels and the production is worth $221.13 a month, but the savings shown are $220, because savings stop at the size of the bill.

## Why does a higher rate help a solar system?

Because the same panel output is worth more money. A system that produces 1,193 kilowatt hours a month is worth $179 at $0.15 and $239 at $0.20, and the hardware costs the same either way. That is why the estimate is not portable between utilities: the same roof, the same panels and the same sunlight produce different savings in two cities, and the difference sits entirely in the tariff.

## Why does a lower rate make the case harder?

The reverse also holds, and it is where an estimate can look wrong to a homeowner who has compared two quotes from two states. A low rate means a longer path to paying the system off, and a system sized to cover a small bill produces little that is worth much. The count of panels does not shrink to compensate: a house still needs the same production to cover the same usage.

## Where does the rate in the estimate come from?

From the utility that serves the city, in the city's own data file on this site. Rates are set per state and per utility, and the Energy Information Administration publishes the average retail price of electricity by state and by sector, which is where the variation is visible. The tariff that governs a specific house is the utility's own rate schedule, including how it treats exports, and that document is worth reading before signing anything.

## Does the rate stay the same for the life of the system?

No. Utility rates change, in both directions, and an estimate is a snapshot at today's rate for a system that will run for decades. The honest way to read the monthly savings figure is against the rate you pay now, not against a forecast of where it goes. If a quote promises savings that only work if rates rise, the rise is the assumption being sold, not the arithmetic.

## What else moves the monthly number?

- The tariff, which is the subject of this text
- The production, which follows from the sunlight, the panel and the tilt
- What the utility credits for exported power, which is usually less than the retail rate
- The share of the bill the system is sized to cover, because savings stop at the bill

## Where do these figures come from?

The rate, the production and the savings come from the Phoenix page, computed from that city's data file by the same function the calculator uses. The Energy Information Administration's explanation of electricity prices and its data browser are the public sources for how prices are set and how they differ by state.
